Hey. I heard you just started as the only designer at your company. Congratulations, really. It's a big deal.

I've been doing this for over five years now, and I've been the only designer more than once. So pull up a chair. I want to tell you a few things nobody told me.

Not to scare you. Just so that when it happens, you'll recognize it. And maybe smile a little instead of panicking like I did.

## Someday, someone will say the sentence

You'll be in a meeting. Maybe a planning call, maybe a quick chat by someone's desk. And someone important will say it, very casually.

"Design is just a reference."

The first time I heard it, I didn't know what to say. I had nine screens open in Figma. I'd spent two weeks on this flow alone. And now they were a reference. Like a mood board. Like a Pinterest pin.

My advice? Don't react in the room. Nod. Write it down. It'll make more sense later, I promise.

## You will be one project

You'll probably work on a product with a web app and a mobile app. When planning starts, watch closely, because this part is fascinating.

Development will get a backend developer, a frontend developer, and a mobile developer. Two projects, three roles. Everyone will agree that makes sense.

Then design will get you. One project.

I know. I tried to do the math too. Here's what I eventually figured out. When you count developers, the work grows to fit the people. When you count designers, the person shrinks to fit the label.

Nobody would ask the backend developer to also build the mobile app because "it's the same product." But you'll get web and mobile, every flow, every state, and keeping the two consistent. All in one tidy line on the roadmap.

Take it as a compliment. You're three people now. You just won't be estimated like it.

What I'd actually do: when you give your estimate, split it. Web, mobile, shared design system. Put the numbers next to each other. You don't need to argue. Let the list do the talking.

## The questions will be postponed

You'll ask good questions. What does the screen show when a new user has no data? What if the API fails? What if someone types a nine-digit amount and it breaks the layout?

And you'll hear, "We'll handle that during development."

Here's how it plays out, so you're ready. The missing decisions become development problems. Whatever ends up inconsistent becomes a design problem. The extra weeks become everyone's problem.

The only thing that won't move is the original estimate. That number is the calmest member of any team. You'll grow to respect it.

What I'd actually do: design the states anyway. Empty, loading, error, the weird edge cases. Put them on their own page in Figma and label them clearly, even if nobody asks. Especially if nobody asks. When the question comes up three weeks into development, and it will, you just send a link.

## Your best work will be invisible

This is the one I wish someone had warned me about.

One day you'll show a screen you're really proud of. Clean, calm, simple. And someone will say, "Nice. That's pretty simple, right?"

You'll smile and say yes. Because it is simple. That was the point.

But you'll know it took eleven versions. You'll remember the flow you redrew because users kept getting lost, and the table you rebuilt because it broke on small laptops. All the tiny decisions that stopped the product from feeling like a pile of random screens.

When good design works, the problem disappears. And once the problem's gone, nobody remembers there was one.

The first time this happened to me, I went home quiet. Not angry, just tired in a way that was hard to explain. If you feel that one day, it's normal. It doesn't mean you did a bad job. It usually means you did a good one.

What I'd actually do: show your thinking, not just your screens. Bring the messy versions to reviews now and then. Say, "Here's what we tried and why it didn't work." People can't value work they never see.

## Don't become the Figma police

Now a bit of honesty, so you don't take the wrong lesson from all this.

You'll be wrong sometimes. I've shipped bad ideas. I've designed things that couldn't be built. Some of the best moments of my career were a developer saying, "This won't work, but what if we tried it this way?" And they were right.

So don't fight for every pixel. Fight for the decisions. Changing a design because the team found something better is great. Changing it because nobody opened the file is the thing worth pushing back on, gently.

And web and mobile aren't two totally separate projects either. They share users, rules, and usually a design system. But sharing a foundation isn't the same as sharing the work. Mobile needs its own navigation and tighter layouts. Web needs denser tables and keyboard shortcuts.

Engineering already knows this. That's why they have three people.

## If you ever get tired of explaining

Keep this one in your back pocket. I've never actually used it. But it's nice to know it's there.

If design is just a reference, then to be consistent, engineering could run the same way. One product. One project. One developer. They take the backend, the web app, and the mobile app, and figure out the gaps along the way.

After all, if the design is just a reference, the code can be one too.

I'm kidding. Mostly.

You're going to be fine. Just be ready. And when you hear that sentence for the first time, text me. I'll understand.
