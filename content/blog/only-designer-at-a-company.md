Hey. I heard you just started as the only designer at your company. Congratulations, really. It's a big deal.
 
I've been doing this for over five years. I've been the only designer in more than one place, and I've swapped stories with a lot of people in the same seat. There's one sentence almost all of us have heard at some point. And if nobody's said it out loud, we've definitely felt it.
 
I want to tell you about it now, so you'll recognize it when it comes.
 
## The day I heard it
 
It was a normal meeting. Nothing special. We were talking about what to build next, and I had a whole flow open in Figma that I'd spent weeks on.
 
Then someone said it, very casually.
 
"Design is just a reference."
 
I didn't know what to say. I just looked at my screen. All those decisions, all those versions, and now it was a reference? Like a mood board?
 
I nodded and wrote it down. I didn't say anything else in that meeting, and honestly, I'm glad I didn't. I would've said something I didn't mean.
 
Here's what I figured out later. Most people don't mean it as an insult. Usually they mean, "We might need to adjust things when we build." That's fair.
 
But the sentence doesn't stay a sentence. It quietly shapes how the work gets planned. And that's the part I want you to watch for.
 
## It shows up in how you're counted
 
Say your product has a web app and a mobile app. When planning starts, watch how the roles get counted.
 
Development usually gets split by the work: backend, frontend, mobile. Everyone agrees that makes sense.
 
Design often gets one line on the roadmap. You.
 
If design is just a reference, that math works. One person makes the reference, and the real work happens somewhere else. But you know that's not what's happening. You're designing web and mobile, every flow, every state, and keeping both consistent.
 
It took me a while to put words to this. When teams count developers, the plan grows to fit the work. When they count designers, the work gets squeezed to fit the person.
 
Nobody's doing this on purpose. Design work is just hard to see from the outside.
 
What I'd actually do: when you give your estimate, split it. Web, mobile, shared design system. Put the numbers side by side. You don't need to argue. Let the list do the talking.
 
## It shows up in the questions that wait
 
You'll ask good questions. What does this screen show when a new user has no data? What if the API fails? What if someone types a really long number and it breaks the layout?
 
And you'll hear, "We'll figure that out during development."
 
That's the same sentence again, just wearing different clothes. If design is just a reference, the details can be sorted out in code.
 
Sometimes that's the right call. But here's how it often goes. The missing decisions turn into development problems. Whatever ends up inconsistent turns into a design problem. And the extra time nobody planned for lands on everyone.
 
The decisions still get made. They just get made in a rush, by whoever happens to be holding the ticket.
 
What I'd actually do: design the states anyway. Empty, loading, error, the weird edge cases. Put them on their own page in Figma and label them clearly, even if nobody asks. Especially if nobody asks. When the question comes up mid-development, and it will, you just send a link.
 
## And sometimes, they're a little bit right
 
Now some honesty, so you don't take the wrong lesson from all this.
 
You'll be wrong sometimes. I've shipped bad ideas. I've designed things that couldn't be built. Some of the best moments of my career were a developer saying, "This won't work, but what if we tried it this way?" And they were right.
 
So don't become the Figma police. Don't fight for every pixel. Fight for the decisions.
 
Changing a design because the team found something better is great. That's design working the way it should. Changing it because nobody opened the file is different, and that's worth a gentle conversation.
 
Can you feel the difference between those two? Once you can, you'll know which battles are worth it.
 
## What I wish I'd said in that meeting
 
It took me years to find the words, so here they are for you.
 
A design file and a codebase are really the same kind of thing. They're both a record of decisions about how the product should work. One lives in Figma, one lives in code.
 
Nobody would call the code "just a reference" and leave the gaps for someone to figure out later. Design deserves that same respect. Not because it's precious, but because it's where a lot of those decisions get made first.
 
You don't have to say this out loud. Just knowing it helps you sit a little taller in those meetings.
 
You're going to be fine. Just be ready. And when you hear that sentence for the first time, text me. I'll understand.
