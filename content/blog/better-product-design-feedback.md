The most common frustration in product design reviews happens when feedback arrives as a prescriptive design directive instead of an observation about user friction.

When a stakeholder says *"Make that green button twice as big and move it above the fold,"* they are trying to solve an underlying concern—usually, *"I’m worried users are missing our primary call to action."*

When feedback focuses on prescribing the layout rather than diagnosing the failure, the team loses the opportunity to explore better solutions.

## Give designers the problem, not the layout

Designers do their best work when they clearly understand what is failing for the user.

Instead of prescribing:
- *"Add a red badge next to the account name."*
- *"Put three more bullet points on the pricing card."*
- *"Move the search input to the left navigation bar."*

Frame the feedback around the struggle:
- *"During testing, users couldn't tell whether their free trial was still active."*
- *"Customers couldn't differentiate between our starter tier and professional tier."*
- *"Users didn't realize they could filter previous invoices by date."*

## The four-question review checklist

Before submitting product review notes, run through these four questions:

1. **What user goal is blocked?** Name the specific task or decision the user is struggling to complete.
2. **What evidence do we have?** Is this based on usability testing recordings, analytics drop-offs, customer support tickets, or team observation?
3. **What is the business context?** Are there timeline constraints, compliance rules, or technical limitations that impact this flow?
4. **Have I separated the symptom from the solution?** Am I describing what is failing, or am I designing the screen in my head?

When product feedback focuses on user clarity and problem definitions, design reviews become collaborative problem-solving sessions rather than battles over personal aesthetic taste.
