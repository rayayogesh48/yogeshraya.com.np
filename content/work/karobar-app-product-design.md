# Karobar: Designing Bookkeeping for People Who Don't Have Time for Bookkeeping

*Making everyday record-keeping faster, simpler, and something small business owners actually stick with.*

Here's something I didn't fully get when I started on [Karobar](https://www.karobarapp.com/): most shop owners don't want a bookkeeping app.

They want to know who owes them money. They want to know if today was a good day. They want to stop digging through a pile of bills, a notebook, and a few WhatsApp messages just to answer one simple question.

The bookkeeping is just the price they pay to get there. And if that price feels too high, they quietly stop paying it.

That gap between what an accounting app expects and how a busy shop actually runs became the thread through all my work. This page is the starting point. It tells the big story, and each deep-dive article linked below picks up one piece of it.

## The product

[Karobar](https://www.karobarapp.com/) is a mobile-first bookkeeping and business-management app from Nepal, also used in Bangladesh. It's built for small shops, wholesalers, restaurants, service providers, and family-run businesses. Most of them don't have an accountant. Many don't have reliable internet.

At its simplest, it's a digital khata. You write down what came in and what went out. But underneath, it grew into a lot more:

1. Sales, purchases, payments, expenses, and quotations.
2. Customers and suppliers, and who owes whom.
3. Inventory and stock levels.
4. Invoices you can print or share on WhatsApp.
5. Reports that turn all of it into a picture of how the business is doing.

It lives on Android, iOS, and the web. And it has to keep working when the internet doesn't.

## My role

I designed [Karobar](https://www.karobarapp.com/) end to end. The mobile app, the web app, and the marketing website all went through me.

The biggest piece was a full revamp. The old app had grown one screen at a time over the years. Menus got deep, older phones struggled, and every new feature was harder to squeeze in. We needed a cleaner foundation that could keep growing.

My work wasn't just about polishing screens. Together with the founders, we shaped how the new [Karobar](https://www.karobarapp.com/) should feel, and how everything in it should be organized. I brought the designs and the thinking, and we made the big calls as a team. Things like what sits on the home screen, how features are grouped, and how someone moves from signing up to recording their first sale.

Honestly, that was the best and hardest part. One hour I'd be fussing over an invoice layout. The next I'd be staring at a messy menu, trying to work out where a feature should even live.

## The challenge

When I first looked at [Karobar](https://www.karobarapp.com/), I thought the work would be about faster forms. Fewer fields, bigger buttons, that kind of thing.

That was part of it. But the real problems were about how people actually run their business, not how an accounting app expects them to.

Our research made that really clear. In the businesses we studied, 72% only recorded totals at the end of the day. Just 17% recorded every single transaction. Only about 30% had a dedicated internet connection. And about a third said they simply didn't have time to keep records.

So these were the questions that kept coming up:

- **Why would I log every sale?** When the shop is busy, writing things down is the first thing to go.
- **What happens when the internet drops?** For a lot of our users, that's not an edge case. It's most days.
- **Who owes me, and who do I owe?** That's the number people check first, and it has to be right.
- **Can my staff help without seeing everything?** An owner might want someone billing customers without seeing purchase prices.
- **How fast can I get to something useful?** Every extra question before the first transaction is a chance to leave.
- **Is my data safe if I lose my phone?** These are business records. Losing them isn't a small thing.
- **Why should I pay for this?** Some existing users didn't even know what premium included.

Here's one that stuck with me. The old signup asked for a lot of business details before you could do anything at all. Someone opening the app for the first time had to fill out forms before seeing a single thing it could do for them. And that's exactly where people dropped off.

In the revamp, we cut it down to three things: personal or business, a business name, and a category. Everything else could wait until later, nudged along by a simple profile-completion bar on the home screen.

## How I approached it

I kept coming back to one question: what is this owner trying to get done before the next customer walks in?

I didn't start with a formal research study. Honestly, I didn't need one. A huge pile of support tickets and customer messages was already sitting there.

People had told us everything. What was broken, what confused them, and what they wished the app could do. So I read through all of it, and that became my map for the revamp.

Then came the revamp itself. One rule mattered more than anything: don't break people's habits. Shop owners used the same flows every single day, so we didn't throw those out. We kept them and made them faster and easier to use.

Everything new had to fit around those habits. Multiple businesses, multiple bank accounts, staff roles, better inventory, clearer reports, and proper printing. Adding all of that without making the app feel heavier was the real puzzle.

Before launch, the team ran a beta with some of our most active users. They worked through real tasks, like adding an item, recording a cash sale, or taking a partial payment.

Their feedback caught things I'd missed. For example, people wanted to send quotations to customers who weren't saved in the app yet. Obvious once you hear it, right? But my design had assumed every quotation already had a customer attached.

Over time, a handful of principles started guiding almost every decision:

1. **Fit the way people already work.** People had built habits around the old app. We made those flows smoother instead of asking anyone to relearn them.
2. **Offline is the default, not the backup.** Recording a sale should never have to wait for a signal.
3. **Ask for less and give more.** Fewer questions up front, and something useful back as soon as possible.
4. **Make money readable at a glance.** "To Receive" and "To Give" belong right on the home screen.
5. **Trust is the product.** Accurate balances, clear permissions, and safe data matter more than any new feature.
6. **Start simple, then grow with the business.** A solo shop and a business with seven staff should both feel at home.
7. **Let people feel the value before asking them to pay.** The basics stay free. Premium is for when the business grows.

## What it taught me

Handling everything on a product this big was hard. The app, the web app, the website, all of it. But I've learned more from it than from anything else I've worked on.

Being the only designer was the toughest part. I was the one person working closely with the founders, so developers and QA came to me for answers. How should this flow work? Why did we decide that? What happens if a user taps this? A lot of my day went into guiding the team, not just designing screens.

But under all of that, the thing that mattered most was simple: thinking like the user. How do they actually use the app? What does a normal day in their shop look like? When do they even get a free minute to open it?

Designing bookkeeping for small businesses isn't really about bookkeeping. It's about making the right thing easy enough that people keep doing it, even on their busiest day.

That's the lesson I carry into everything I design now.
